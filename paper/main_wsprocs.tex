\title{In-Domain Accuracy Does Not Predict Cross-Device Variability:
A Pre-Registered Multi-Cohort Benchmark for Wearable Atrial Fibrillation Screening}

\author{Urmi Shah}
\address{Department of Computer Engineering, San Jose State University,\\
San Jose, CA, USA\\ E-mail: urmimanishkumar.shah@sjsu.edu}

\begin{abstract}
Atrial fibrillation (AFib) is the most common sustained arrhythmia and a major,
largely preventable cause of ischemic stroke; a large share of cases are silent
and undiagnosed. Consumer wearables that record a single-lead electrocardiogram
(ECG) could surface these cases at population scale, but a screening model must
run unchanged on the heterogeneous fleet of devices people already own. We ask
what in-domain held-out accuracy---the metric on which such models are routinely
selected---actually tells us about off-device behaviour. We contribute a
pre-registered, leakage-safe benchmark that scores every model on \emph{identical}
unseen windows from four external cohorts spanning three hardware classes, with
paired DeLong tests and patient-clustered confidence intervals. On a
patient-grouped hold-out from a hospital 12-lead corpus (CPSC~2018, $n{=}1{,}376$
records, Lead~I only) a deep convolutional--recurrent network (CNN-LSTM) is
modestly and reliably ahead of a lightweight device-agnostic model built only
from heartbeat-timing features (AUC $0.949$ vs.\ $0.935$; across $20$ alternate
hold-out splits the deep model is significantly better in $14$, mean
$\Delta$AUC~$0.021$). That in-domain advantage does not transfer in any
predictable way. Across the four external cohorts the deep model's AUC ranges
from $0.642$ to $0.935$ (SD~$0.121$) while the timing model stays between $0.792$
and $0.873$ (SD~$0.041$)---a $2.9\times$ difference in spread---and the sign of
the gap flips by cohort, favouring the timing model on Apple~Watch ($+0.151$) and
MIT-BIH Holter ($+0.098$), tying on AliveCor ($-0.001$), and favouring the deep
model on a second Holter cohort ($-0.062$). Because the two Holter cohorts
disagree, this variability is not explained by device class alone. A model
additionally trained across devices is strongest wherever it can be scored
without train-on-test contamination. In-domain accuracy therefore predicts
neither which model will lead on an unseen device nor how far it will fall: for
screening deployment, multi-cohort variability is the quantity that must be
reported. An exploratory onset-prediction analysis (104 patients) yields only
modest, sub-clinical discrimination (AUC~$0.66$) and is reported as such. All
numbers come from a single pre-registered run and are released for reproduction.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{atrial fibrillation; single-lead ECG; wearable screening; cross-device

\section{Introduction}
Atrial fibrillation is the most common sustained arrhythmia and a leading cause
of ischemic stroke, yet a large fraction of cases are paroxysmal, silent, and
undiagnosed until a stroke occurs. The clinical benefit of finding AFib is
concrete and time-sensitive: timely anticoagulation substantially reduces stroke
risk~\cite{perez2019appleheart}. Because so many cases are asymptomatic, the
bottleneck is not treatment but \emph{detection at scale}, and consumer
wearables---smartwatches that record a single-lead ECG (for example, Apple~Watch
Series~4 and later)---put an opportunistic screening instrument in tens of
millions of hands. The AFib signature (absent P~waves and irregular beat-to-beat
timing) is visible in a single lead, so single-lead screening is feasible in
principle.

The obstacle we address is a \emph{deployment} problem specific to screening.
A screening model does not run on one curated device; it must run, unchanged, on
whatever hardware a population already wears---different sampling rates, gains,
electrode geometries, and noise profiles. Models trained on one recording device
frequently degrade sharply on another because they latch onto hardware-specific
waveform characteristics rather than the underlying cardiac pattern; we call this
the \emph{cross-device gap}. For population screening, robustness to this gap is
not a secondary metric---it determines who actually gets caught.

This paper asks a question about \emph{evaluation} rather than architecture: does
in-domain held-out accuracy---the metric on which wearable screening models are
routinely selected---predict which model will still work once it leaves its
training device? Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A \textbf{cautionary benchmark finding}: a small, reliable in-domain
  advantage carries no information about off-device behaviour. The deep model is
  significantly better in-domain on most hold-out splits, yet across four external
  cohorts its AUC varies almost three times as much as the device-agnostic model's,
  and the sign of the gap between them flips from cohort to cohort.
  \item Evidence that \textbf{device class does not explain the variability}. Two
  ambulatory Holter cohorts---the same hardware category---place the two models in
  opposite orders, so ``cross-device gap'' is not a property of the device alone
  and cannot be predicted by grouping cohorts by hardware.
  \item A \textbf{pre-registered, leakage-safe benchmark protocol} for wearable
  AFib screening---frozen patient-grouped hold-out, frozen thresholds, zero-shot
  external cohorts scored on identical windows, a pre-declared family of paired
  significance tests with patient-clustered confidence intervals, and a 20-split
  sensitivity analysis---directly responsive to documented leakage and
  reproducibility problems in medical machine learning~\cite{kapoor2023leakage}.
  \item An \textbf{honest exploratory study} of AFib \emph{onset} prediction from
  pre-onset sinus rhythm, reported with its limitations.
\end{enumerate}
We scope the work to AFib detection and stroke prevention; it does not address
heart-attack (myocardial infarction) prediction, a different condition with a
different ECG signature.

\section{Related Work}
Single-lead AFib detection has been studied since the PhysioNet/CinC 2017
challenge on short AliveCor recordings~\cite{clifford2017af}, and large
prospective work established that smartwatch screening surfaces undiagnosed
AFib~\cite{perez2019appleheart}. Recent deep models report very high in-domain
accuracy, and---directly relevant to us---Ben-Moshe et
al.~\cite{benmoshe2024rawecgnet} show that a carefully trained raw-ECG network can
be made to \emph{generalize} across leads and cohorts, and argue that raw-waveform
models can outperform hand-crafted RR-interval features. We do not dispute this:
with cross-device training and enough data, learned representations can close the
gap. Our claim is narrower and complementary. First, the realistic screening
constraint is \emph{zero-shot} transfer---a deployed model rarely gets retrained
per device---and under that constraint we find timing features transfer without
any cross-device training. Second, we make the comparison on \emph{matched} data
with a pre-registered paired test, rather than comparing models trained on
different corpora. Bahrami~Rad et al.~\cite{bahramirad2024crowdsourced} similarly
report that timing-based features transfer across AliveCor and Apple~Watch better
than learned waveform features; we add a same-data statistical comparison and a
multi-cohort, clustered-CI cross-device evaluation.

Methodologically, our design responds to the leakage-driven reproducibility
crisis in ML-based science~\cite{kapoor2023leakage}: we pre-register the hold-out
and tests before training. Onset prediction has a long history beginning with the
PAF Prediction Challenge~\cite{moody2001paf}; we revisit it on continuous Holter
data with patient-grouped evaluation and treat it as exploratory.

\section{Data and Methods}

\subsection{Datasets}
We use five public or personally exported sources. CPSC~2018 (within the
PhysioNet/CinC~2020 corpus~\cite{alday2020classification}) provides hospital
12-lead ECGs, from which we take Lead~I only. PhysioNet/CinC~2017 provides
single-lead AliveCor recordings~\cite{clifford2017af}. The MIT-BIH Atrial
Fibrillation Database (\texttt{afdb}) and the Long-Term AF Database
(\texttt{ltafdb}) provide annotated ambulatory Holter
recordings~\cite{moody1983afdb,petrutiu2007ltaf,goldberger2000physionet}. A small
set of personal Apple~Watch exports (54 recordings, four volunteers, one confirmed
AFib) is used only for real-world validation; its labels are the watch's own
classifier output, not independent clinical adjudication. A separate tabular
clinical risk dataset is used for an additional analysis (Section~\ref{sec:extra})
and is reported as a distinct cohort.

\subsection{Models}
The \textbf{device-agnostic model} (RR~+~RF) detects AFib from heartbeat-timing
features only---coefficient of variation, RMSSD, pNN50, sample entropy, and
related statistics of the RR-interval series---fed to a random forest. Because
these features measure timing rather than waveform shape, they are insensitive to
device sampling rate and gain. The \textbf{deep model} (CNN-LSTM) is a three-block
1D convolutional encoder followed by a two-layer LSTM and a linear head, trained
on 10-second Lead~I windows.

\subsection{Pre-registered evaluation protocol}
To avoid comparing models trained on different data, and to guard against the
test-set leakage that undermines reproducibility in medical
ML~\cite{kapoor2023leakage}, we fixed the analysis before training. We created a
deterministic, label-stratified hold-out of CPSC records (20\%, fixed seed) after
an explicit one-record-per-patient check, and excluded these records from
\emph{both} models' training. The device-agnostic model and a CPSC-only deep model
were then scored on the identical hold-out. Decision thresholds were frozen on
training/validation data, never on the hold-out. The primary discrimination test
is the paired DeLong test on AUC~\cite{delong1988comparing,sun2014fast}; the
primary threshold-level test is McNemar's test on correctness vectors. A single
pre-registered Holm family~\cite{holm1979simple} (DeLong plus fixed-threshold
McNemar across the model-versus-model pairs) controls multiple comparisons.
Class imbalance during training was handled with SMOTE applied \emph{inside} each
cross-validation fold~\cite{chawla2002smote}. A pre-registered amendment adds the
cross-device family below; the same-data primary is unchanged.

\subsection{Zero-shot cross-device evaluation}
The screening question is answered by moving the \emph{already-trained} models,
with no retraining or fine-tuning, onto unseen hardware. For each external cohort
we score RR~+~RF and the CPSC-only CNN-LSTM on the \emph{identical} 10-second
Lead~I windows, so the comparison is truly paired: long Holter records are cut
into consecutive 10-second windows and each window is labeled by the active rhythm
at its midpoint. Per cohort with both classes present we run a paired DeLong test
of RR~+~RF versus CNN-LSTM(CPSC); these cross-device tests form a single
pre-registered Holm family, separate from the same-data family. Every external AUC
is reported with a \textbf{patient-/record-clustered bootstrap} 95\% CI (resampling
whole patients, since windows within a patient are correlated). The
data-asymmetric combined CNN-LSTM is scored only on cohorts it never saw during
training (it is omitted for CinC~2017 to avoid train-on-test leakage).

\subsection{Split-sensitivity (secondary)}
As a labeled secondary sensitivity analysis we repeat the entire
hold-out\,$\rightarrow$\,train\,$\rightarrow$\,paired-eval chain over 20 alternate
hold-out seeds, each in an isolated run so the pre-registered seed-42 primary is
untouched, and report the distribution of $\Delta$AUC and DeLong $p$. This does not
replace the primary; its purpose is to show how much the single-split conclusion
depends on the split.

\subsection{Exploratory onset prediction}
Separately, we test whether imminent AFib can be predicted from sinus rhythm. From
continuous Holter records we label 30-second sinus windows as \emph{pre-onset}
(onset within 30~minutes) or \emph{control} (no onset within 60~minutes), excluding
windows already in AFib. Features combine RR/HRV statistics with
neurokit2~\cite{makowski2021neurokit} atrial-ectopy and expanded HRV markers, each
z-scored against the patient's own control-window baseline. The pre-registered
primary is the neurokit-feature variant; RR-only and P-wave variants are reported
as labeled sensitivity analyses. Evaluation uses patient-grouped cross-validation
pooled over \texttt{afdb} and \texttt{ltafdb}.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Off-device behaviour is variable and unpredictable (main result)}
Figure~\ref{fig:variability} and Table~\ref{tab:crossdevice} report the zero-shot
head-to-head on identical windows across four external cohorts.

Two facts stand out. First, the \emph{spread} differs sharply: the deep model's
AUC ranges from $0.642$ to $0.935$ (SD~$0.121$) while the timing model stays
within $0.792$--$0.873$ (SD~$0.041$), a $2.9\times$ difference in standard
deviation and $3.6\times$ in range. Its worst cohort is also far worse
($0.642$ vs.\ $0.792$). Second, the \emph{direction} of the gap is not stable:
the timing model leads on Apple~Watch ($+0.151$) and MIT-BIH ($+0.098$), the two
tie on AliveCor ($-0.001$), and the deep model leads on Long-Term AF ($-0.062$).

Critically, the two Holter cohorts---MIT-BIH and Long-Term AF, the same hardware
class---place the models in \emph{opposite} orders. Whatever drives the
variability is therefore not device class alone; cohort composition (patient mix,
AFib burden, recording length and quality) matters at least as much. We flag this
because it undercuts a natural but unsafe inference: that one can characterise a
model's ``cross-device gap'' by testing on one representative device per class.

\emph{Statistical caveats.} The MIT-BIH cohort contributes $84{,}295$ windows from
only $23$ patients. DeLong's variance estimator assumes independent samples, so
its nominal $p$ (numerically $0$ there, $z{=}51$) is badly anticonservative under
within-patient correlation. We therefore treat the patient-clustered bootstrap CIs
as the primary inferential quantity and report DeLong as a secondary, clearly
non-independent statistic. The Apple~Watch cohort contains a single confirmed
positive, so its DeLong test is undefined and its AUC is a plausibility check, not
a discrimination claim. Long-Term AF could not be scored exhaustively
($84$ records $\times\sim24$\,h $\approx725{,}000$ windows exceeded the wall clock
on three attempts); we evaluate a seeded, uniformly random subsample of at most
$300$ windows per record ($25{,}197$ windows, $84$ records), which preserves
record identity for clustered CIs and is recorded in the released results.

\subsection{Controlled same-data comparison}
On the pre-registered hold-out (Table~\ref{tab:ecg}) the deep model is ahead by
$0.013$ AUC, which on this single split does not reach significance (95\% CI
$-0.032$ to $0.006$; DeLong $p{=}0.33$, McNemar $p{=}0.40$, Holm-corrected). The
20-split sensitivity analysis shows that this particular split understates a real
effect: over alternate hold-outs the deep model is ahead by a mean $\Delta$AUC of
$0.021$ (median $0.021$; 2.5--97.5\% interval $-0.008$ to $0.040$) and the
difference is significant at $\alpha{=}0.05$ in $14$ of $20$ splits. We therefore
do \emph{not} claim the two models are equivalent in-domain: the deep model holds
a small but reproducible in-domain advantage, and the pre-registered seed happens
to be one of the six splits where it does not reach significance. This makes the
cross-device result stronger, not weaker---the model with the reliable in-domain
edge is the one whose off-device performance is least predictable.

The combined deep model, additionally trained on wearable data, reaches AUC
$0.975$ in-domain, but this is a deployment comparison with more and different
training data, not a same-data test.

\subsection{Exploratory onset prediction}
Predicting AFib before it begins is a harder, separate problem. Pooled across two
Holter cohorts (104 patients, patient-grouped cross-validation), personalized
timing and atrial-ectopy features give modest, consistent pre-onset discrimination
(neurokit variant, window AUC $0.66$). At about $0.5$ false alarms per hour the
model warns of a minority of onsets a median of $\sim$1--2 minutes ahead.
Per-patient normalization was essential. We report this as a real but sub-clinical
signal, not a deployable early-warning system; the RR-only ($0.606$) and P-wave
variants are sensitivity analyses.

\subsection{Additional analysis: tabular clinical risk (separate cohort)}
\label{sec:extra}
For completeness we also train tabular clinical risk models (random forest, AUC
$0.954$; gradient boosting, AUC $0.927$) on a distinct patient population. These
are \emph{not} combined with the ECG models: the cohorts do not overlap, so any
fused score would be unvalidated. We therefore report them as a separate cohort
and treat clinical$+$ECG fusion as out of scope until same-patient paired data are
available.

\section{Discussion}
The practical message is about \emph{model selection under uncertainty}, not about
which architecture is better. The deep model earns a small, reproducible in-domain
advantage---significant on 14 of 20 hold-out splits---and on one external cohort
it is clearly the better choice. Yet its off-device AUC spans $0.642$ to $0.935$,
almost three times the spread of the device-agnostic model, and nothing in the
in-domain evaluation indicates which cohorts will land at which end of that range.
A team selecting a screening model on in-domain accuracy would pick the deep
model and would be right some of the time and badly wrong at others, with no way
to tell the cases apart in advance.

We want to be careful about what this does \emph{not} show. It is not evidence
that simple features beat deep learning: on Long-Term AF the deep model wins by
$0.062$, and a model trained across devices is strongest wherever it can be scored
honestly---consistent with work showing raw-ECG networks generalize well when
trained appropriately~\cite{benmoshe2024rawecgnet}. Nor is it a clean
``device-shift'' story. Our two ambulatory Holter cohorts belong to the same
hardware class and rank the models in opposite orders, so device identity is not
sufficient to explain or predict the gap; cohort composition---patient mix, AFib
burden, recording length and signal quality---evidently matters as much. This is
itself a caution for benchmark design: testing on one dataset per device class
would have produced a confident and wrong generalization from either Holter cohort
alone.

What follows practically is modest but concrete. Wearable screening evaluations
should report performance across several external cohorts and quote the spread and
the worst case, not a single external number; a model whose off-device behaviour is
predictable may be preferable to one with a higher expected value and a long tail,
particularly when per-device validation is impossible after deployment.

Several limitations bound these claims. Four external cohorts cannot separate the
many factors that co-vary with them, so we identify variability without
attributing it. The MIT-BIH evidence rests on 23 patients and the nominal DeLong
$p$ is anticonservative under within-patient correlation; we report clustered CIs
for this reason. Long-Term AF is evaluated on a seeded subsample of at most 300
windows per record rather than exhaustively. The Apple~Watch cohort has one
confirmed positive and device-generated labels, so it supports plausibility only
and contributes the largest single $\Delta$ in the table on the weakest evidence.
The tabular clinical models use a different population and are not a cross-modal
ablation. The onset analysis uses cohorts of selected AFib patients and shows only
modest discrimination; richer atrial markers, longer pre-onset context, and
purpose-built data are the natural next steps.

\section{Conclusion}
In-domain accuracy is a poor guide to how a wearable AFib screening model will
behave on hardware it has not seen. In our pre-registered benchmark the model with
the reliable in-domain advantage is also the one whose external performance is
least predictable, varying almost threefold more across cohorts and changing rank
from cohort to cohort---including between two cohorts of the same device class. We
contribute the protocol that makes this visible: identical windows, zero-shot
external cohorts, paired tests, patient-clustered intervals, and a 20-split
sensitivity analysis. For screening tools that cannot be revalidated on every
device they reach, we argue for reporting cross-cohort variability and worst-case
performance alongside headline accuracy. Predicting AFib before it begins remains
hard; our personalized features yield a real but sub-clinical early-warning signal
that motivates future work rather than a deployable alarm.
